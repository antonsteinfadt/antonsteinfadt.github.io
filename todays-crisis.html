Yesterday’s Solution and Today’s Crisis

7. November 2026

Mathematics and philosophy have long been close allies. Their respective influences on one another have always been reciprocal: philosophical reflections reinforced or limited mathematical speculations, and conversely, mathematical advances and reflections also influenced the development of philosophy. Great figures in our field—such as Leibniz, Descartes, and Pascal—were also great philosophers. Over time, however, increasing specialization and a growing emphasis on technical problem-solving weakened this once-close bond. That is unfortunate. For regardless of how closely mathematics and philosophy collaborate institutionally, it will always be true that mathematics raises questions it cannot answer using its own methods (after all, there is no mathematical proof that mathematics should be done with chalk on a blackboard forever or that mathematical beauty matters more than practical usefulness). Fortunately, the two disciplines seem to have been converging again in recent months. As Jeremy Avigad put it on August 24 of this year:

“A curious side effect of the AI revolution is that mathematicians everywhere are being turned into philosophers, as we are forced to come to terms with our mathematical values.”1

So should we all become more philosophical? YES! I think this would be a desirable development, and not just with regard to mathematical values. The need for such philosophical reflection becomes particularly evident in times of mathematical crisis. Indeed, it was during the first major crisis of mathematics around 1900, when the certainty of mathematical knowledge was shaken, that the link between mathematics and philosophy experienced a (temporary) revival. In the resulting controversy, the major schools of logicism (Frege, Russell, Whitehead), formalism (Hilbert), and intuitionism (Brouwer, Heyting) debated the nature and foundations of our science. If the First Crisis brought mathematics and philosophy closer together, perhaps the time has come for a similar revival.

I think it is no exaggeration to describe the current phase as a kind of “Second Crisis of Mathematics”, and I believe this will be an appropriate term for our history books. In fact, I also believe that the two crises are closely related: our past achievements—that is, the responses and solutions to the “First Crisis”—have paved the way for the second. It was the formalization of mathematical reasoning that laid the groundwork for its mechanization, and thus for today’s crisis in mathematical practice. Indeed, the possibility of mechanizing mathematical reasoning was already heavily discussed by Hilbert, von Neumann, Gödel, and others during the First Crisis.2

In the table below, I would like to compare the two crises across various dimensions and, in conclusion, offer some recommendations for dealing with our current crisis.

I Origins and Nature of the Crises

The foundations of mathematics come under pressure: naive set theory, infinity, permissible forms of inference, etc. How certain is mathematics? Can we trust our mathematical reasoning at all? On what foundations should mathematics be built and taught? What do mathematical truths mean? etc.

The foundations of mathematical practice come under pressure: What does it mean to be a mathematician? What do mathematicians do? What are the values of mathematics? What remains of mathematical research when proof generation is fully automated? How will mathematicians be trained at all? etc.

The triggers are internal mathematical and logical problems, especially antinomies and the limits of formal systems.

The trigger is a technological change: AI takes over activities that have until now distinguished mathematicians.

II Debate and Institutions

Academic drivers (internal): mathematicians themselves dispute which foundations and methods should define the discipline.

Commercial drivers (external): tech companies can exert considerable influence; mathematicians vs. AI companies?

The debate involved an influential but relatively limited circle of researchers in foundations and philosophically engaged mathematicians (Weyl: set-theoretic antinomies were commonly viewed as “Grenzstreitigkeiten” [“peripheral conflicts”]).3

The debate cuts across the entire discipline: conferences, institutes, blogs, academic journals, social media, etc.

Charged language and heated debate: “revolution” (Weyl), “attempted coup” and “dictatorship of prohibitions” (Hilbert), warnings of the “dissolution of analysis”, Hilbert–Brouwer controversy, etc. Hilbert wrote in 1904: “hier stehen sich gegenwärtig noch die verschiedensten Meinungen der Forscher schroff einander gegenüber.” (“Here, the most diverse opinions of researchers still stand in stark opposition to one another.”)3

Similarly dramatic language and debate: from a “golden age” to the “end of mathematics” (also: “the Mathocalypse”, “takeover”, “a tsunami of maths”; the Caltech Mathathon controversy; numerous appeals, open letters, and public statements).

Radical demands: abandon classical forms of inference, and with them parts of classical mathematics, if they cannot be constructively justified (Brouwer).

Radical demands: fundamentally restructure established professional roles, reward systems, research practices, etc.

III Proving and Understanding

Formalization as translation: informal mathematics is to be transferred into explicit symbolic systems.

Translation back becomes the problem: formally correct proofs must be translated back into terms that humans can understand.

Trust: trust is to shift from naive mathematical evidence to explicitly secured formal foundations.

Trust: formal correctness is no longer the final word; new sources of error in formalization, bugs in Lean, soundness of logical systems, etc.

Hilbert’s program → theoretical incompleteness: not everything that is mathematically true is provable within a sufficiently strong formal system (Gödel).4

AI mathematics → “practical incompleteness”: not everything that has been proved can still be understood and integrated into the shared body of mathematical knowledge.

Central challenge: formalizing the whole of mathematics, proving the consistency of its formal systems by finitary means, and finding a general decision procedure for first-order logic.6

Central challenge: verification, explanation, “digestion”, contextualization, peer review, and canonization of AI-generated mathematics (attention and understanding become scarce).

Progress as the solution of ever more major problems (e.g., Hilbert’s list of 23 problems; “We must know. We will know.”).

Progress? Drawing on Thurston's essay, a “humanistic perspective” on mathematical progress is making a comeback, one that focuses primarily on understanding and community. A growing body of theorems need not imply a corresponding increase in understanding.

“But solving problems is only a tool and proxy for achieving the primary goal of conceptual understanding and insight.”

IV The self-conception of mathematics

Logicism, formalism, and intuitionism compete over the proper foundations.

Competing conceptions of what mathematics is, what it should be, and what it is practiced for.

Implicit rules of practice had to be made explicit. What had we been assuming all along without putting it into words?

Explicit rules must be supplemented by their implicit practice (the values, goals, etc., of research). What had we valued all along without having to say so?

The Creating Subject: For Brouwer, mathematics is essentially mental construction; symbolic representations are subordinate to this activity.

The intrinsic value of doing mathematics: What significance does the experience of grasping a problem in depth have? The question of the subject returns under technological conditions.

Mathematical education: Formal and axiomatic methods raise new questions about how rigor and mathematical proof should be taught (Think of the axiomatic textbooks that would later be produced by Bourbaki!)

Mathematical education: How can students develop mathematical understanding and independent judgement when AI can perform the reasoning for them?

Philosophy of mathematics becomes important!

Philosophy of mathematics becomes important!

V New Perspectives and Outcomes

New fields: the crisis gives rise to, or strengthens, mathematical logic, proof theory, and research on foundations.

New fields: the AI crisis is likely to generate new research fields and practices (“Math 2.0”). There are already interesting projects!

Metamathematics: Mathematics itself becomes an object of mathematical investigation, particularly the consistency, completeness, and limits of formal systems.

Metapractice: The reader may think of something analogous. There is, for example, “Philosophy of Mathematical Practice”. Also, a new kind of “Metamathematics” would be interesting.

Learning from crisis …

Such a historical perspective is important for at least two reasons: Firstly, I believe that we can learn a great deal from the “First Crisis”, and secondly, because it strengthens our confidence that mathematics will survive the present crisis as well. Several lessons from the first crisis seem particularly relevant to our present situation (but there are many more!). To begin with, the first crisis in mathematics teaches us that crises can indeed have a positive outcome. Mathematics emerged from that first crisis stronger and on a more solid foundation, and we should hope for the same today. In other words, crises can be productive and bring to light problems that already existed. Secondly, I think the first crisis teaches us that it is counterproductive to become fixated on certain ideas or practices. Formalistic tendencies may have prevailed in the long run, but intuitionism and neologicism remain fruitful fields of research or have spurred further developments. Yes, Hilbert’s original program may have failed as intended, but it helped give rise to modern proof theory. Similarly, ideas from intuitionistic logic found new applications through the Curry–Howard correspondence, linking proofs to programs and influencing modern type theory and proof assistants.

Thirdly, looking back, the Hilbert–Brouwer controversy reminds us how easily disagreements can turn into institutional power struggles. We should therefore ensure that the present debate remains open and inclusive, and that the future direction of mathematics is shaped by the mathematical community itself. Views and sentiments within our community vary widely, as Kevin Buzzard’s essay “To grieve, or not to grieve?” helpfully illustrates.5 These are challenging times for each and every one of us. We should act responsibly, mindful of our traditions, to the best of our knowledge and belief. In this context, a particularly troubling feature of the present crisis – which has no parallel in the first one – is the influence of external commercial actors for whom the long-term flourishing of mathematics need not be a primary objective. Frustration should be directed less at AI itself (as a technology) than at providers that promised to “empower mathematicians”, but then compete with their own users – while simultaneously expecting us to validate their output without compensation! The mathematical community should resist this appropriation of its expertise and defend its autonomy through independent infrastructure and open science.

We can’t go back – but no mathematician shall be left behind in this transition. So let’s step up to the challenges and carry the torch of mathematics ever forward!
